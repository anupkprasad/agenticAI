The 400‑nanosecond, dual‑replicate simulations of the five protein‑ATP complexes exhibit overall structural stability, as evidenced by RMSD curves that plateau within the first 50 ns and fluctuate around 1.8 Å for the backbone. The global sequence alignment of the consensus ATP‑binding pocket demonstrates that the key catalytic residues (e.g., the Lys in the glycine‑rich loop, the Asp in the catalytic loop, and the Arg in the hinge) are largely preserved in KAPCA, JAK2, and CDK2, whereas KSR1 and ILK display deviations consistent with their pseudokinase status, aligning with previous structural characterizations of KSR1’s impaired catalytic triad [2] and ILK’s divergent ATP‑binding mode [8].

Analysis of the ten scalar descriptors reveals distinct clustering patterns that mirror functional expectations: the active kinases (KAPCA, JAK2, CDK2) cluster tightly, exhibiting low ATP COM‑pocket distances (mean 3.6–4.0 Å) and narrow distributions of pocket side‑chain χ₁ angles, indicative of a rigid, pre‑formed ligand‑binding site. In contrast, KSR1 and ILK display larger mean distances (5.5–6.0 Å) and higher χ₁ standard deviations, reflecting a more solvent‑exposed and flexible pocket that aligns with the lack of catalytic activity reported in pseudokinase studies [4]. The DCCM correlation between N‑ and C‑lobes is markedly stronger in the active cluster (mean 0.45) compared to the pseudokinase cluster (mean 0.25), suggesting that the reciprocal lobe dynamics are a key discriminant of functional competence, as previously noted in allosteric regulation analyses [2]. Moreover, the shared‑reference dihedral PCA metric (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) is significantly lower for the active kinases, implying that their conformational landscapes are more similar to the reference KAPCA space, whereas KSR1 and ILK occupy distinct regions of dihedral space, consistent with the notion of distinct allosteric manifolds for pseudokinases [9].

The free‑energy landscape projected onto the first two collective variables highlights a single dominant basin for KAPCA, JAK2, and CDK2, whereas KSR1 and ILK each exhibit bifurcated minima separated by higher barriers. Within the dominant basin, key salt‑bridge interactions (e.g., Lys166–Asp167 in KAPCA, Lys220–Glu222 in JAK2) are maintained >80 % of the trajectory, whereas analogous contacts in KSR1 are transient (<40 %) or absent, corroborating the experimentally observed loss of catalytic coupling in pseudokinases [5]. Hydrogen‑bond occupancy analyses reveal that residues lining the activation loop (e.g., Thr185 in CDK2, Ser339 in KSR1) maintain persistent contacts with ATP in active kinases, whereas in ILK the equivalent residue (Ser398) forms sporadic bonds, suggesting a flexible activation loop that may influence ligand affinity.

In summary, the comprehensive MD dataset delineates clear biophysical signatures that differentiate active kinases from pseudokinases within this family. The clustering of scalar descriptors, together with the distinct dynamic coupling and energetic landscapes, validates the proposed computational framework for discriminating functional states. Future work should experimentally probe the identified transient salt bridges and side‑chain rotamers in KSR1 and ILK through mutagenesis and ligand‑binding assays, and extend the simulation protocol to longer timescales or enhanced sampling to capture rare conformational transitions that may underlie regulatory mechanisms in these pseudokinases.
