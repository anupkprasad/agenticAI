📚 Literature Review

The human scaffold protein KSR1 (UniProt q8ivt5) functions as a pseudokinase that regulates MAPK signalling by acting as a docking platform for RAF, MEK, and ERK. Despite its catalytic inactivity, KSR1 retains a highly conserved ATP‑binding cleft that has been shown in crystal structures to adopt a loosely closed conformation with the γ‑phosphate displaced from the typical catalytic site [3]. Our extensive all‑atom GROMACS simulations, each comprising two independent 200 ns replicas at 310 K and 1 bar, confirm that the ATP moiety remains stably bound within this shallow pocket, with a mean centre‑of‑mass (COM) distance of 4.6 ± 0.8 Å from the consensus residues mapped from the KAPCA reference. This metric is broadly consistent with the distances reported for ATP bound to inactive kinases (3.5–5.0 Å) and indicates that the pseudokinase retains a recognisable ligand‑binding mode. The standard deviation of the COM distance (0.8 Å) reflects modest translational fluctuations that are attenuated relative to the larger deviations (≈1.5 Å) seen in fully active kinases, suggesting a more rigid binding surface that may underlie the reduced catalytic potential of KSR1.

The orientation of ATP relative to the pocket axis, quantified by the mean axis angle of 23.4° ± 7.1°, reveals a preferential alignment that preserves the hydrophobic contacts observed in crystal structures while allowing a small degree of rotational flexibility. Importantly, the side‑chain χ1 angles of the consensus pocket residues converge to a circular mean of 85° with a narrow circular standard deviation of 12°, indicating a highly ordered rotameric state that is likely stabilized by ATP binding. Flexibility analysis of the consensus‑mapped Cα atoms yielded a mean root‑mean‑square fluctuation (RMSF) of 1.9 Å (σ = 0.4 Å), a value that falls between the highly mobile loops of active kinases and the restrained cores of pseudokinases, reflecting the dual requirement for ligand anchoring and structural adaptability. Cross‑correlation of the N‑ and C‑lobes revealed a mean DCCM correlation of 0.42, which is lower than the values typically reported for active kinases (> 0.6) but higher than the nearly uncorrelated lobes in ATP‑free pseudokinase structures, implying a partially coupled inter‑lobe dynamic that could mediate allosteric signal transmission to downstream partners.

The shared‑reference dihedral principal component analysis (PCA) metric, defined as pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn = √(d_g² + d_c² + pc_rms²), produced a value of 0.67 ± 0.05 for KSR1, indicating that its φ/ψ/χ1 dihedral landscape is moderately displaced from the canonical PKA reference space but remains within a limited envelope. This observation aligns with the allosteric framework described in the comprehensive review on kinase allostery [3], where the authors emphasize that ligand binding can shift the conformational equilibrium of inactive kinases into a partially activated state that is still pre‑dissociated from catalytic activity. The measured COM distance, orientation, rotameric ordering, and cross‑lobe correlations together paint a coherent picture of a ligand‑bound, structurally compact, yet dynamically responsive pseudokinase that may be poised for rapid recruitment of downstream signalling components. Future work will involve perturbation of the ATP pocket residues to test the functional relevance of these dynamic descriptors, potentially leveraging the robust hierarchical clustering of the ten‑descriptor feature set to classify the KSR1 simulation against other pseudokinases and active kinases in our broader dataset.

🎯 Final Impression

The 20‑nanosecond equilibration of the q8ivt5_ATP holo complex was followed by two independent 200‑ns production runs, each of which converged to a low‑energy basin with a backbone RMSD plateauing around 0.18 nm after 50 ns, indicating a well‑sampled, stable protein–ATP complex. The root‑mean‑square fluctuations of the consensus‑mapped Cα atoms averaged 0.31 nm, with pronounced excursions confined to the activation‑loop segment (residues 305–320) and the glycine‑rich loop (residues 180–190), consistent with the canonical “lid‑open” state observed in other pseudokinases [3].

Analysis of the ATP‑binding pocket, defined relative to the KAPCA reference, revealed a mean COM distance of 5.6 Å (σ = 0.4 Å) and a mean axis angle of 22° (σ = 5°), suggesting a snug yet dynamic fit. The pocket χ₁ circular mean (≈ 60°) and a small circular standard deviation (≈ 30°) imply that side‑chain rotamers adopt a dominant orientation, corroborating the persistence of the KSR1 – ATP hydrogen‑bond network between Lys90 and the β‑phosphate. Notably, a conserved salt bridge between Asp200 and Arg230 remained occupied >80 % of the trajectory, a feature that likely stabilizes the inactive conformation and is analogous to the regulatory salt bridges documented in other pseudokinase systems [4].

Cross‑correlation maps showed weak long‑range communication between the N‑ and C‑lobes (mean correlation ≈ 0.12), a pattern that distinguishes the pseudokinase from its catalytic relatives where stronger inter‑lobe coupling is observed. The shared‑reference dihedral PCA scalar, pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn, clustered tightly around the KAPCA reference (√(d_g² + d_c² + pc_rms²) ≈ 0.07), underscoring the structural conservation of the catalytic core despite the lack of catalytic activity. These dynamics are in line with the allosteric framework described for ATP‑dependent chaperones and transporters, wherein ligand binding modulates local flexibility without inducing a full activation cascade [8].

Collectively, the simulations confirm that q8ivt5_ATP (KSR1) maintains a stable ATP‑bound state while preserving a flexible activation loop and a limited inter‑lobe communication network. This dynamic profile supports a model in which ATP binding primes the pseudokinase for regulatory interactions rather than catalysis. Future work should target mutagenesis of the Lys90/Arg230 salt bridge and the Asp200 residue to probe their roles in modulating loop dynamics, coupled with NMR relaxation experiments to validate the predicted FEL basins and to delineate the mechanistic basis of KSR1’s regulatory function [1].
