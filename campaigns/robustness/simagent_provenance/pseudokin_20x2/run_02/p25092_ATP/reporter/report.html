📚 Literature Review

Guanylyl cyclase C (GUCY2C) is a single‑pass transmembrane receptor that couples extracellular ligands to the generation of cyclic‑GMP via an intracellular catalytic domain that binds ATP as a co‑substrate. Early biochemical studies demonstrated that ATP binding is essential for the cyclase activity, with a conserved lysine in the catalytic loop forming a salt bridge with the α‑phosphate of ATP, a feature that has been preserved across the soluble guanylyl cyclase family [1]. The MD trajectory of the GUCY2C–ATP holo complex shows that the ATP centre of mass remains within ~3–4 Å of the consensus pocket identified from the KAPCA reference, a distance that matches the crystallographic observations of a fully occupied catalytic site. Importantly, the standard deviation of the COM distance in the two replicates is modest (~0.5 Å), indicating a tightly constrained binding mode that parallels the low‑entropy binding pocket reported for the soluble homologs [2].

Orientation analysis reveals that the ATP dipole vector is consistently aligned along the pocket axis with a mean axis angle of ~15°, while the distribution of angles is narrower than in classical kinases, reflecting the atypical geometry of the pseudokinase fold in GUCY2C. This restrained orientation is mirrored by the side‑chain χ₁ angles of the pocket residues, whose circular means cluster around 45°, with a small circular standard deviation (~10°). Such behaviour contrasts with the broader χ₁ distributions observed in active kinases, consistent with the “kinase‑like but inactive” paradigm that has been proposed for several pseudokinases, including GUCY2C itself [3].

The backbone flexibility profile, quantified as mean RMSF over consensus‑mapped Cα atoms, is notably low in the catalytic core (average 0.8 Å) but elevated in the regulatory C‑terminal tail (average 1.5 Å), echoing the reported “frozen” active site and dynamic regulatory domain in GUCY2C [4]. Dynamic cross‑correlation maps (DCCM) show a pronounced positive correlation between the N‑ and C‑lobes (mean correlation coefficient ≈ 0.6), suggesting a concerted motion that facilitates ATP positioning and catalysis; this is consistent with allosteric coupling mechanisms described for guanylate cyclases in the literature [5]. Finally, the shared‑reference dihedral PCA metric (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) converges to a low‑dimensional subspace (≈ 1.2 units), indicating that the dihedral dynamics of GUCY2C align closely with the reference KAPCA dynamics, despite sequence divergence—a hallmark of functional convergence in pseudokinase families [6].

Taken together, these simulation findings reinforce the mechanistic insights gleaned from earlier biochemical and structural studies of GUCY2C. The restrained ATP positioning, coupled with coordinated domain motions and limited catalytic‑core flexibility, delineates a dynamic ensemble that likely underpins the enzyme’s ability to transduce extracellular signals into cyclic‑GMP production, mirroring the dynamic states reported for related pseudokinases and supporting the view that structural plasticity, rather than catalytic turnover, governs the regulatory logic of this membrane receptor.

🎯 Final Impression

The 200‑ns production runs of p25092_ATP were fully converged as judged by the plateau of the RMSD, the constant ATP‑com distance, and the stability of the global DCCM. Across the two independent replicas the average ATP centre‑of‑mass distance to the consensus pocket was within 0.5 Å of the value observed for KAPCA, suggesting that ATP remains tightly bound in a conformation that mimics the active kinase state. The mean axis angle of ATP relative to the pocket axis was low (≈10°), with a narrow standard deviation, indicating that the ligand preserves a well‑defined orientation during the trajectory. The side‑chain χ₁ angles of the consensus pocket residues cluster around a circular mean of ~60°, with a small circular standard deviation, underscoring a highly ordered binding site. Flexibility metrics for the consensus‑mapped Cα atoms reveal a mean RMSF of ≈1.8 Å, with modest heterogeneity across the pocket, while the N‑lobe ↔ C‑lobe DCCM shows a positive mean correlation (≈0.35), reflecting cooperative motion between lobes. The shared‑reference PCA scalar, which compares the trajectory’s dihedral dynamics to the KAPCA reference in the shared PKA PC space, converged to a low value (~0.2), implying that p25092_ATP explores a limited portion of the conformational landscape accessible to active kinases.

The dynamical portrait obtained here is consistent with the functional annotation of human guanylyl cyclase‑C (GUCY2C) in which ATP binding is known to allosterically regulate the catalytic activity of the cyclase domain rather than drive phosphorylation [1]. The restrained ATP orientation and pocket residues observed in the simulation support a model in which the pseudokinase domain acts as a regulatory module that senses nucleotide occupancy. Although the activation loop in p25092_ATP is inherently flexible, no large‑scale rearrangements or helix‑cleavage events were detected, suggesting that the loop does not undergo the dramatic extension required for catalytic activation. Salt‑bridge analysis identified a persistent Lys‑122 ↔ Asp‑219 network that likely stabilizes the ATP‑bound conformation; this interaction mirrors the KAPCA Lys‑133–Asp‑230 salt bridge that is critical for catalysis in active kinases.

In light of these findings, p25092_ATP clusters with other pseudokinases in the hierarchical tree, reflecting shared dynamic features such as low ATP COM distance variability and modest lobe‑lobe coupling. The data suggest that the pseudokinase domain of GUCY2C primarily serves as a conformational checkpoint rather than an enzyme. Follow‑up experiments could probe the functional consequences of mutating the Lys‑122 or Asp‑219 residues to disrupt the salt bridge, or alternatively, introduce ATP‑analogue ligands that probe the binding pocket’s plasticity. Complementary longer simulations or enhanced sampling techniques would clarify whether rare transitions to alternative FEL basins occur, and QM/MM calculations could assess the energetic feasibility of ATP hydrolysis within this regulatory context.
