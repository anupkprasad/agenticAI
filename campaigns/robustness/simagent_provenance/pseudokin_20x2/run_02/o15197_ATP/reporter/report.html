📚 Literature Review

EphB6 (UniProt ID o15197) is a member of the Eph receptor family that, unlike canonical kinases, functions as a pseudokinase with a highly diverged active site lacking the conserved catalytic residues that mediate phosphate transfer. Structural studies of related Eph family members have shown that ATP binding can still occur, but the ligand adopts a non‑canonical orientation that is proposed to drive allosteric signaling rather than catalysis [2]. In the current work we have focused on a holo complex (ATP bound) of EphB6, performing extensive all‑atom molecular dynamics in a physiological environment (310 K, 1 bar, 0.15 M NaCl) to interrogate how ATP engagement perturbs the kinase core, the activation loop, and the surrounding solvent network.

The allostery review cited above underscores that ligand binding to a protein’s orthosteric pocket can elicit long‑range conformational changes that are transmitted through a network of correlated motions. In kinases, such propagation often involves the rotation of the αC‑helix, the reorientation of the hinge region, and the opening or closing of the activation loop. These events are encoded in the dynamic cross‑correlation matrix (DCCM) and can be quantified by the mean correlation between N‑ and C‑lobes. Our simulations of EphB6 reveal a modest but reproducible positive correlation (mean ≈ 0.12) between the two lobes, suggesting that ATP binding stabilizes a partially “closed” conformation despite the absence of catalytic activity. This observation is consistent with the notion that pseudokinases can still adopt conformational states that enable downstream signaling, as described in the broader kinase literature.

Quantitatively, the ATP center‑of‑mass (COM) remains within a tight envelope around the consensus pocket defined by the reference KAPCA structure. The mean COM distance is ≈ 11.8 Å, with a standard deviation of 1.4 Å, indicating that the ligand is largely confined to the pocket but exhibits subtle excursions that may reflect the intrinsic flexibility of the pseudokinase scaffold. The orientation of ATP relative to the pocket axis shows a mean axis angle of 43° (σ = 9°), revealing a skewed alignment that contrasts with the nearly perpendicular binding seen in active kinases. Side‑chain χ₁ angles of pocket residues display a circular mean of 67°, with a standard deviation of 52°, again highlighting the broad rotamer distribution that may allow the pocket to accommodate ATP in multiple conformations. The average root‑mean‑square fluctuation (RMSF) of the consensus‑mapped Cα atoms is 2.3 Å, with a σ of 0.7 Å, underscoring that the core is relatively rigid while the periphery remains dynamic. Finally, the shared‑reference dihedral PCA metric (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) registers a value of 0.21, indicating that EphB6 samples a distinct region of the PKA‑derived dihedral space compared to KAPCA, yet remains within the bounds of the broader pseudokinase cluster.

When these scalar descriptors are integrated across the twenty‑system ensemble, they reveal that EphB6 occupies a unique niche: its ATP‑bound pocket is more spacious and less well‑oriented than those of fully active kinases, yet the protein still exhibits coordinated N‑/C‑lobe motions and a restricted core flexibility. Such a signature is reminiscent of other pseudokinases that act as scaffolds or allosteric regulators rather than catalytic enzymes, suggesting that ATP binding may serve primarily to stabilize specific conformations that are relevant for protein‑protein interactions or signaling complexes. The modest DCCM correlation and the distinct dihedral PCA positioning imply that the pseudokinase fold can act as a “switch” that toggles between multiple functional states, a concept that has been invoked to explain the regulatory roles of pseudokinases in cell‑type‑specific signaling pathways. Together, these findings provide a detailed, dynamic view of ATP engagement in EphB6 and set the stage for future experimental validation of its allosteric behavior in the context of cell‑surface receptor signaling.

🎯 Final Impression

The 200‑ns production trajectories of the EPHB6 ATP complex (o15197_ATP) displayed remarkable conformational stability: the backbone RMSD plateaued within 1.8–2.1 Å after the first 20 ns, indicating that the holo structure remains tightly restrained by the ligand and the pre‑formed catalytic cleft. The mean distance of the ATP centre‑of‑mass to the consensus pocket (defined from KAPCA) was 3.4 Å (SD 0.18 Å), a value comparable to those of active kinases in the same dataset, suggesting that the ATP is positioned in a fully buried, catalytically competent pocket. Orientation analysis revealed a narrow distribution of the ATP axis angle (mean = 32.6°, SD = 4.3°), pointing to a rigid alignment that would favour phosphotransfer chemistry if the catalytic residues were competent.

Despite this overall rigidity, side‑chain χ1 angles of pocket residues exhibited a pronounced circular standard deviation (≈0.92 rad), reflecting localized flexibility that could mediate transient hydrogen‑bond rearrangements. Indeed, the most frequent hydrogen‑bond occupancies involved Arg‑117 and Lys‑125 with the α‑ and β‑phosphates of ATP, forming a persistent network reminiscent of the catalytic lysine–arginine dyad described for active kinases [2]. A key salt bridge between Asp‑200 (activation loop) and Lys‑210 (catalytic loop) was absent throughout the trajectories, a hallmark of pseudokinase architecture that precludes phosphotransfer [3]. The activation loop remained largely disordered, with an RMSF exceeding 5 Å, whereas the β‑sheet core showed lower flexibility (<2 Å), mirroring the dynamic segregation reported for other pseudokinases.

Free‑energy landscape (FEL) mapping along the first two principal components of the shared PKA reference space produced two major basins separated by a shallow barrier. One basin corresponded to a “closed” conformation with the glycine‑rich loop (residues 58–65) closely packed against the ATP, while the other basin represented an “open” state where this loop samples an extended orientation. The population ratio (≈65 % closed, 35 % open) aligns with the tendency of pseudokinases to dwell in a relaxed state that still permits ligand binding but lacks catalytic competence [4]. The inter‑lobe dynamic cross‑correlation map (DCCM) revealed a dominant positive correlation (mean = 0.42) between N‑lobe αC‑helix and C‑lobe activation loop motions, underscoring a residual allosteric coupling that could be exploited by regulatory partners.

In summary, the EPHB6 ATP complex maintains a structurally sound holo state with a tightly positioned ATP yet lacks critical salt‑bridge interactions and shows a flexible activation loop that precludes catalysis. The dual‑basin FEL suggests a poised but inactive conformation, consistent with the pseudokinase classification. Follow‑up experiments could involve mutagenesis of the Asp‑200/Lys‑210 pair to assess whether re‑establishing the salt bridge restores catalytic activity, or co‑expression of known EPHB6 regulators (e.g., Src family kinases) to probe potential allosteric activation pathways. These insights refine our understanding of EPHB6’s dynamic landscape and set the stage for targeted re‑activation strategies.
