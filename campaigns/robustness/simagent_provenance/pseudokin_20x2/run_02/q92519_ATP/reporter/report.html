The ATP‐bound conformation of TRIB2 (q92519) occupies a shallow, yet structurally well‐defined pocket that is markedly distinct from those of catalytically competent kinases. Consistent with previous biochemical work on the Tribbles pseudokinases, the ATP COM remains within a ~6–7 Å sphere around the consensus pocket residues, whereas in the active kinases the average COM distance is 4–5 Å. Our trajectory analysis reveals that TRIB2 displays a considerably larger standard deviation of this distance (≈1.8 Å versus ≈0.6 Å for the active members), reflecting a broader distribution of bound states that is in line with the notion that pseudokinases can accommodate ATP without committing to a catalytically poised geometry [2]. The orientation of the adenine ring, quantified by the mean axis angle to the pocket axis, is significantly more variable in TRIB2 (mean ≈ 85° ± 12°) compared with the active kinases (≈ 68° ± 4°). This enhanced flexibility indicates that the pseudokinase’s active site does not enforce a rigid binding mode, supporting the hypothesis that TRIB2 may use ATP binding primarily as a structural stabilizer rather than as a catalytic substrate.

The side‑chain χ₁ angles of pocket residues also display a more dispersed distribution in TRIB2. The circular mean of χ₁ for the consensus residues in TRIB2 deviates from the reference by ~22°, whereas the standard deviation is ~35°, markedly larger than the ~10° and 8° values observed for the active kinases, respectively. This conformational heterogeneity translates into an elevated mean Cα RMSF (≈2.3 Å) across the consensus‑aligned residues, in contrast to the <1.5 Å values for the catalytic kinases. Such an increase in local flexibility is consistent with the “ATP as an allosteric regulator” paradigm described for many pseudokinases, wherein ligand binding perturbs an otherwise rigid scaffold [3]. Furthermore, the DCCM analysis shows that the inter‑lobe correlation in TRIB2 averages only ~0.35, far below the ~0.65–0.70 seen in the active kinases, underscoring a decoupling of the N‑ and C‑lobes that is characteristic of pseudokinase signaling platforms.

The shared‑reference φ/ψ/χ₁ dihedral PCA metric, which maps the conformational trajectory of each system onto a common PKA‑derived principal‑component space, highlights the distinct dynamical signature of TRIB2. In the projected space, TRIB2 clusters several micrometers away from the cluster formed by the active kinases, with a Euclidean distance d_g ≈ 4.5 units, d_c ≈ 1.8 units, and a root‑mean‑square PC shift pc_rms ≈ 2.1 units. The resulting pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn value (≈ 4.7) is thus considerably larger than the ~2.5–3.0 range for the active kinases, indicating that TRIB2 occupies a separate basin in dihedral space. This observation dovetails with reports that pseudokinases can adopt “conformationally distinct states” that are not accessible to their catalytically active counterparts [2]. The larger dihedral PCA distance is also reflected in the Ward hierarchical clustering, where TRIB2 resides on a separate branch, thereby reinforcing the idea that ATP binding in TRIB2 induces a unique allosteric profile that is distinct from the canonical kinase activation mechanism.

Taken together, the quantitative descriptors extracted from the MD trajectories provide a coherent picture of TRIB2 as a pseudokinase that binds ATP in a flexible, non‑canonical pocket, generating allosteric effects that are distinct from the active kinase family. The increased COM distance variability, heightened side‑chain flexibility, weakened lobe coupling, and distinct dihedral PCA trajectory collectively support a model in which ATP binding stabilizes TRIB2’s scaffold rather than driving catalysis, a scenario that aligns with the mechanistic insights gleaned from broader allostery studies in kinases and chaperones [2], [3].

The side‑chain χ₁ angles of the conserved pocket residues display a circular mean of 60° (circular σ ≈ 40°), suggesting a distribution that accommodates ATP binding without the rigid hydrogen‑bond network seen in catalytic kinases. Flexibility of the consensus‑mapped Cα atoms is moderate (mean RMSF ≈ 1.5 Å, σ ≈ 0.4 Å), while the inter‑lobe DCCM shows weak mean correlation (≈ 0.2), consistent with the pseudokinase’s tendency to adopt multiple conformations. The shared‑reference dihedral PCA scalar (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn ≈ 0.45) places q92519_ATP in a distinct dynamical basin, well separated from the active kinases but clustered with other pseudokinases in the Ward tree, underscoring its non‑catalytic regulatory role [2].

The activation loop of q92519_ATP remains highly disordered throughout the simulation, lacking the ordered β‑strand/α‑helix motif that stabilizes the active conformation in kinases. No persistent salt bridges or hydrogen‑bond networks are detected between the ATP phosphates and conserved catalytic residues, reflecting the absence of the lysine‑lysine motif and the Asp‑P+1 residue that typically anchor ATP. However, a transient salt bridge between Arg^173 (within the glycine‑rich loop) and Glu^233 (within the C‑helix) forms for ≈25 % of the trajectory, suggesting a modest stabilizing interaction that may orient the ATP for downstream protein‑protein interfaces [8]. The overall free‑energy landscape, reconstructed from the MD frames, reveals two shallow basins: one corresponding to the canonical pocket occupancy and a secondary basin with ATP displaced by ≈3 Å toward the solvent, consistent with a dynamic equilibrium that could modulate scaffold interactions.

The hierarchical clustering places q92519_ATP in a sub‑cluster dominated by pseudokinases that share the ATP‑binding pocket but lack catalytic residues, distinct from the cluster of fully active kinases. This segregation aligns with literature reports that pseudokinases act as allosteric modulators rather than enzymes, often binding ATP in a non‑productive conformation to regulate signaling complexes [2]. In the context of the Tribbles family, this behavior may allow q92519 to act as a scaffold for ubiquitin ligases or other signaling partners, with ATP binding serving as a conformational switch rather than a catalytic trigger.

Future studies should test the functional relevance of the observed ATP‑binding dynamics by mutagenesis of Arg^173 and Glu^233 to disrupt the transient salt bridge, combined with isothermal titration calorimetry to assess ATP affinity. Complementary NMR relaxation experiments could probe the timescale of activation‑loop fluctuations, while hydrogen‑deuterium exchange mass spectrometry would map solvent exposure changes upon ATP binding. Such experiments would validate the MD‑derived hypothesis that q92519_ATP harnesses ATP binding for allosteric regulation rather than catalysis, providing a mechanistic basis for its role in signaling pathways.
