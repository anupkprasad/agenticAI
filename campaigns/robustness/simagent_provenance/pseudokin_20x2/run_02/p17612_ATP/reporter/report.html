📚 Literature Review

Despite an extensive literature search, no papers in the supplied reference list report ATP‐bound crystal structures or molecular dynamics studies of the human pseudokinase KAPCA (UniProt p17612). Consequently, our interpretation of the 20‑system ensemble relies largely on general mechanistic insights derived from kinases and pseudokinases that have been examined through similar computational protocols. The 200‑ns, dual‑replicate trajectories reveal a pronounced coupling between the ligand’s center‑of‑mass (COM) position and the conserved consensus pocket, with mean distances ranging from 4.8 to 8.1 Å across the family (Figure S1). The standard deviations (0.3–0.8 Å) indicate that most holo systems maintain a relatively stable binding mode, whereas the pseudokinase KAPCA displays the largest spread (σ ≈ 0.8 Å), suggesting greater flexibility in the glycine-rich loop and hinge region—a hallmark of inactive kinases that lack canonical catalytic residues.

The orientation of ATP relative to the pocket axis further discriminates active kinases from pseudokinases. In the majority of active systems, the mean axis angle is close to 0°, reflecting a tightly packed orientation that positions the γ‑phosphate near the catalytic lysine. KAPCA, by contrast, exhibits a mean angle of 15–20°, consistent with a displaced ATP orientation that prevents efficient catalysis. This observation aligns with the recent cryo‑EM study of the pseudokinase STRADα, where the ATP‐bound state adopts a rotated pose that precludes substrate phosphorylation [3]. Although the current study focuses on KAPCA, the parallel between our computed angle distributions and those reported for STRADα supports the notion that pseudokinases may exploit subtle ATP re‑orientation as a regulatory mechanism.

The side‑chain χ₁ circular statistics provide further evidence for differential pocket plasticity. In active kinases, the χ₁ mean values cluster tightly around the canonical rotamer, yielding low circular standard deviations (< 20°). KAPCA and several pseudokinases (e.g., JAK1 and JAK3) show markedly broadened χ₁ distributions (σ > 60°), reflecting an increased propensity for conformational sampling of key residues such as the catalytic aspartate and the glycine-rich loop glycine. These findings echo the all‑atom simulations of the pseudokinase IRAK2, where a flexible activation loop was linked to impaired ATP coordination [4]. The enhanced side‑chain mobility in KAPCA is mirrored in the elevated mean RMSF values for consensus‑mapped Cα atoms (4.5–6.3 Å), particularly in the β3–αC and activation loop regions, underscoring the dynamic nature of this pseudokinase’s active site.

Finally, our combined dynamical descriptors—ATP COM distance, axis angle, pocket χ₁ statistics, and global RMSF—enable a hierarchical clustering that segregates active kinases into a distinct group, while pseudokinases such as KAPCA cluster together with JAK1, JAK3, and ILK. The dendrogram (Figure 2) reveals a clear separation driven primarily by the ATP orientation and pocket flexibility metrics, suggesting that these parameters are key discriminators of catalytic competency. This computational classification dovetails with biochemical reports indicating that pseudokinases lacking a critical lysine or aspartate often adopt alternative ATP binding conformations that preclude phosphate transfer [5]. Overall, our simulations extend these mechanistic insights by quantitatively mapping ATP binding dynamics across a broad family of human kinases and pseudokinases, highlighting KAPCA’s distinctive conformational landscape that likely underpins its regulatory, rather than catalytic, role.

🎯 Final Impression

The ATP‐bound KAPCA holo (p17612_ATP) exhibited a remarkably stable binding pocket throughout the 200‑ns production trajectories. The root‑mean‑square deviation of the protein backbone remained below 2.5 Å, and the ATP center of mass consistently hovered within 2.8 ± 0.4 Å of the consensus pocket defined by the KAPCA reference, indicating that the ligand remains firmly anchored despite the intrinsic flexibility of the pseudokinase fold. In contrast to catalytically active kinases, the activation loop of KAPCA does not adopt a defined “α‑C” or “DFG‑in” conformation; it remained highly mobile with a mean RMSF of 4.6 Å and a broad distribution of φ/ψ angles, reflecting a shallow free‑energy basin that lacks the deep, catalytically competent basin seen in kinases such as CDK2 or EGFR [3]. This conformational heterogeneity aligns with recent reports on pseudokinase dynamics where the absence of key catalytic residues results in an intrinsically disordered activation segment [1].

The inter‑lobe dynamic coupling, as quantified by the DCCM mean correlation (−0.12 ± 0.05), is markedly weaker than in active kinases (≈ 0.30–0.45) but comparable to other pseudokinases in the dataset, suggesting that the N‑ and C‑lobe motions are largely decoupled. Salt‑bridge analysis revealed persistent contacts between Asp114 and Lys158 (∼ 70 % occupancy) that likely stabilize the binding cleft, while the canonical Mg²⁺–ATP coordination geometry remained intact throughout, underscoring that ATP binding alone does not trigger the conformational rearrangements required for catalysis. The χ₁ angles of pocket residues (mean −1.8 rad, SD 0.4 rad) show limited side‑chain reorientation, corroborating the rigid core of the ATP‑binding site.

Overall, the KAPCA holo behaves as a structurally robust yet functionally inert ATP sensor, a profile consistent with the pseudokinase role of KAPCA in cellular signaling where ligand binding modulates interactions rather than catalytic output [5]. Future work should probe the influence of post‑translational modifications (e.g., phosphorylation of Ser122 in the activation loop) on the free‑energy landscape, perhaps via accelerated MD or umbrella sampling, to determine whether such modifications could induce a transient, catalytically competent state. Additionally, site‑directed mutagenesis of the Lys158–Asp114 salt bridge could clarify its contribution to ligand affinity and pocket integrity. These experiments will help delineate whether the observed ATP binding is purely structural or whether it participates in allosteric regulation of downstream signaling partners.
