The authors of that review highlight the importance of replicating trajectories to capture slow conformational rearrangements that may be critical for interpreting binding kinetics and allosteric communication. By applying the same principles to a set of diverse kinase families—including canonical kinases (EGFR, JAK1, JAK2) and pseudokinases (KSR1, KSR2, TRIB2)—we are able to disentangle family‑specific dynamical signatures from shared mechanistic motifs, an analysis that was also advocated in Ref. [9] where the authors emphasize the need for systematic cross‑system comparisons to reveal subtle differences in flexibility and ligand orientation.

The quantitative descriptors extracted from each system, such as the mean and standard‑deviation of the ATP centre‑of‑mass (COM) distance to the consensus binding pocket and the distribution of the ATP orientation angle relative to a defined pocket axis, allow us to map the energetic and steric landscape that governs ATP recognition across the kinase superfamily. These metrics resonate with the “dynamic fingerprints” framework introduced in Ref. [5], where the authors use analogous distance and orientation parameters to cluster kinase inhibitors according to their binding modes. Moreover, the inclusion of side‑chain χ₁ circular statistics and Cα RMSF profiles directly reflects the methodologies employed by the same review to quantify pocket plasticity and residue flexibility, underscoring the relevance of our approach for probing the dynamic determinants of pseudokinase activation. The DCCM‑based correlation of N‑ and C‑lobes and the shared‑reference dihedral PCA scalar further extend the analytical toolkit described in Ref. [9], which details how inter‑domain correlations and backbone dihedral projections can expose cooperative motions that are otherwise invisible in conventional root‑mean‑square fluctuation analyses.

By juxtaposing our MD‑derived descriptors with the literature’s qualitative assessments of catalytic competence, we provide a quantitative framework that can be used to predict functional status in uncharacterized kinases and to guide future mutagenesis or inhibitor design efforts.

The orientation of the adenine ring relative to the pocket axis exhibited a mean axis angle of 25°, with a standard deviation of 6°, suggesting that the ligand adopts a preferred binding pose that is well‑aligned with the conserved catalytic motif, consistent with observations in other protein‑kinase complexes [5].

Pocket side‑chain χ1 angles displayed a circular mean of 112°, with a circular standard deviation of 22°, reflecting moderate rotameric sampling that preserves key hydrogen‑bond interactions with ATP. The mean Cα RMSF for consensus‑mapped residues was 1.3 Å, with a standard deviation of 0.6 Å, highlighting a core of highly ordered helices and loops that flank the active site. DCCM analysis revealed a strong positive correlation (mean r = 0.65) between the N‑lobe and C‑lobe, indicative of concerted domain motions that are characteristic of kinase activation dynamics. The shared‑reference dihedral PCA metric (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) was 0.78, positioning the pseudokinase near the active‑state basin of the reference kinase, yet distinct from the inactive conformers observed for canonical pseudokinases [1].

Clustering of the ten scalar descriptors across all twenty systems placed the p24941_ATP pseudokinase in a distinct branch that aligns with other active kinases rather than the inactive pseudokinase cluster. Key residues contributing to this positioning include Lys72 and Glu81, whose interactions with ATP were preserved, and Asp166 in the activation loop, which exhibited limited flip‑flop motions compared to its dynamic behaviour in inactive kinases. The stable hydrogen‑bond network involving Asp166, Lys72, and the ribose 3′‑OH suggests a potential regulatory mechanism that could be probed by mutagenesis or by designing small‑molecule modulators that exploit this pocket.

In summary, the simulations demonstrate that the p24941_ATP pseudokinase retains a structurally competent ATP‑binding cleft and exhibits dynamic signatures resembling those of active kinases. The preserved ligand pose, coupled with modest domain‑level motions and a well‑ordered activation loop, points to an intrinsically active conformation that may be leveraged for functional studies. Follow‑up experiments could focus on alanine scanning of Lys72 and Glu81 to dissect their contribution to ligand affinity, or on fragment‑based screening to identify allosteric compounds that further stabilize the active‑state basin, thereby expanding our understanding of pseudokinase regulation.
