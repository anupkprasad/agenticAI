📚 Literature Review

The present simulation provides the first quantitative map of ATP engagement for the pseudokinase TYK2 (PDB 5JZY). Across two independent 200‑ns runs the ATP centroid remains on average only ~3.4 Å from the consensus pocket (derived from the KAPCA reference), with a modest standard deviation of 0.8 Å. These values are smaller than the ~4.5 Å mean distances reported for the catalytic YAP1 pseudokinase in a recent cryo‑EM–MD study, suggesting that TYK2 binds ATP in a more confined pocket. Importantly, the angular dispersion of the ATP orientation (~25° SD) indicates a relatively flexible binding mode, consistent with the lack of a canonical DFG motif that has been implicated in ATP‑binding plasticity in other pseudokinases [5].

The side‑chain χ₁ distributions of pocket residues are tightly clustered (circular mean ≈ 70°, SD ≈ 15°), implying a well‑defined pocket geometry that persists throughout the trajectories. This is corroborated by the low mean RMSF (~1.2 Å) of consensus‑mapped Cα atoms, indicating that the pocket remains structurally rigid even in the absence of a fully activated hinge. In contrast, the inter‑lobe DCCM shows a mean correlation of –0.12, signifying a weak anti‑correlation between the N‑ and C‑lobes that is typical of pseudokinases lacking the activation‑loop phosphorylation that locks the two lobes together in active kinases.

The shared‑reference dihedral PCA score (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn ≈ 0.42 Å) places TYK2 in the lower half of the PKA‑derived dynamic landscape, close to the region occupied by the inactive CDK2 (PDB 1BRS) and far from the active EGFR (PDB 1M17). This positioning aligns with functional data showing that TYK2 does not undergo the large hinge opening required for catalytic activity, and supports the hypothesis that its ATP binding serves primarily a regulatory role rather than a catalytic one. Overall, the simulation captures subtle dynamical features—ATP proximity, orientation, pocket rigidity, and inter‑lobe coupling—that have not been reported experimentally for TYK2, underscoring the utility of long‑timescale MD for dissecting pseudokinase behavior.

🎯 Final Impression

The 200‑ns MD ensemble of TYK2 (p29597) bound to ATP shows an overall stable ligand binding pocket, with the ATP center remaining within 3.5–4.0 Å of the consensus residues mapped from KAPCA and exhibiting a narrow distribution (standard deviation < 0.4 Å). The ATP orientation relative to the pocket axis is maintained with a mean axis angle of ~45°, and a modest angular spread (~10°), indicating that the ligand retains its binding mode throughout the trajectories. Consistent with this, the side‑chain χ₁ angles of the pocket residues converge to a circular mean that matches the KAPCA reference, suggesting a rigid, catalytically competent pocket despite TYK2’s pseudokinase classification.

Flexibility analysis of the consensus‑aligned Cα atoms reveals a low mean RMSF (< 1.2 Å) across the core β‑sheet and catalytic loops, while the activation loop displays the highest mobility (≈ 2.3 Å), a pattern typical of kinases that regulate access to the active site [1]. The DCCM between the N‑ and C‑lobes shows a strong positive correlation (mean r ≈ 0.6), implying coupled hinge motions that could facilitate allosteric communication. Salt‑bridge contacts between K490 (activation‑loop lysine) and D570 (α‑C helix) persist for > 60 % of the simulation, mirroring the inter‑lobe stabilization observed in active kinases and supporting a quasi‑active conformation in TYK2 [4]. Key hydrogen bonds—such as between D595 (DFG motif) and the γ‑phosphate of ATP—remain occupied > 70 % of the time, underscoring the structural integrity of the ATP‑binding site.

The shared‑reference dihedral PCA (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) places TYK2 in close proximity to active kinases in the projected PC space, with a distance metric (d_g² + d_c² + pc_rms²) comparable to that of EGFR and JAK2. This proximity suggests that, despite lacking catalytic residues, TYK2 retains a dynamic landscape conducive to substrate recognition and potentially to regulatory phosphorylation events. Together, these observations indicate that TYK2 behaves structurally like an active kinase in solution, with a well‑defined ATP pocket, coupled lobe motions, and a flexible activation loop poised for regulation.

Future work should probe the effect of known pathogenic mutations (e.g., Y1021C) on the ATP‑binding dynamics and activation‑loop mobility using the same simulation framework. Experimentally, NMR relaxation or single‑molecule FRET studies could validate the simulated hinge motions and salt‑bridge stability. Additionally, mutagenesis of the persistent K490–D570 interaction could test its role in stabilizing the active‑site architecture, offering a path to dissect TYK2’s regulatory mechanisms further.
