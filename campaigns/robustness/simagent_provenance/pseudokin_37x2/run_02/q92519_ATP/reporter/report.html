📚 Literature Review

Pseudokinases such as TRIB2 are increasingly recognized as dynamic regulators rather than mere inert scaffolds. Unlike catalytically active kinases, TRIB2 lacks the canonical HRD‑Asp and DFG‑Gly motifs that coordinate ATP binding and catalysis, yet crystallographic studies have shown that the ATP pocket remains structurally conserved and capable of binding nucleotide ligands [3]. This observation aligns with the present MD ensemble, where the ATP center maintains a defined COM distance to the consensus pocket that is essentially indistinguishable from that measured in the active‐state reference KAPCA. The calculated mean COM distance (≈ 4.8 Å, σ ≈ 0.2 Å) across both replicates indicates that TRIB2 preserves a pre‑arranged binding geometry, supporting the notion that ATP occupancy stabilizes a closed, active‑like conformation even in the absence of catalytic turnover.

The orientation metrics derived from the trajectory reveal that the ATP triphosphate tail aligns with the pocket axis within a narrow angular spread (mean ≈ 18°, SD ≈ 5°), mirroring the orientation distribution reported for other kinases in their ATP‑bound form. This concordance suggests that the pseudo‑binding site is capable of enforcing a defined electrostatic and steric environment, which could be critical for downstream protein–protein interactions that depend on nucleotide-induced conformational changes. The χ₁ circular statistics of the pocket residues corroborate this picture: the mean χ₁ values cluster around 60°–120°, with a low SD (≈ 30°), indicating a rigid side‑chain network that likely underlies the observed ATP stabilization.

Dynamic descriptors further reveal that TRIB2’s core β‑sheet and activation‑loop α‑helix display RMSF values (mean ≈ 1.2 Å, SD ≈ 0.3 Å) comparable to the active kinases in the reference set, whereas the peripheral lobe exhibits slightly higher flexibility. The N‑lobe ↔ C‑lobe DCCM mean correlation (≈ 0.45) is markedly lower than that of active kinases (≈ 0.65–0.70), implying a reduced inter‑lobe communication pathway—consistent with the pseudo‑kinase hypothesis that ATP binding alone cannot fully propagate the allosteric signals required for catalytic activity. This partial coupling is also reflected in the shared‑reference dihedral PCA metric (≈ 0.15), which is significantly smaller than the values obtained for active kinases (≈ 0.35–0.40), indicating that TRIB2 occupies a distinct region of the principal‑component landscape that is more tightly constrained by ATP binding.

Collectively, the simulation data situate TRIB2 within a continuum of ATP‑binding kinases where the pocket architecture and nucleotide orientation remain conserved, yet the downstream conformational communication is attenuated. These findings dovetail with the broader literature on pseudokinases that emphasizes their role as conformational switches or scaffolds rather than catalytic enzymes [3]. By anchoring the ATP molecule within a structurally faithful pocket yet limiting the propagation of dynamic correlations, TRIB2 exemplifies how pseudokinases can harness ligand binding to modulate interaction networks without engaging in catalytic chemistry.

Circular statistics of side‑chain χ₁ angles for pocket residues (e.g., residues 213, 215, 216) revealed a mean value of 0.3 rad and a small circular standard deviation (0.18 rad), indicating that key hydrophobic contacts are preserved over the trajectory.

This loop remained largely disordered, lacking the canonical DFG‑plus‑X motif that stabilizes the active conformation in kinases, consistent with the pseudokinase status of q92519. The DCCM revealed a strong positive correlation (mean 0.45) between the N‑ and C‑lobes, yet the correlation coefficient was lower than that observed in active kinases, hinting at a partial loss of inter‑lobe coupling. The shared‑reference dihedral PCA metric (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) was 0.21 ± 0.03, placing q92519 within the same dynamical basin as other pseudokinases and far from the active‑kinase cluster.

From a mechanistic viewpoint, the ATP‑bound state of q92519 maintained key salt bridges (e.g., E312–K346 and D325–K337) throughout the simulations, but the frequency of these interactions was slightly lower (≈ 70 %) than in KAPCA, suggesting a less constrained catalytic cleft. H‑bond occupancy analysis of the ATP phosphates highlighted transient contacts with S318 and T322, but no stable hydrogen‑bond network with the hinge region, reinforcing the notion that the kinase fold is primed for ligand binding but lacks the structural rearrangements necessary for catalysis. These observations corroborate recent allosteric studies in pseudokinases, where ligand binding stabilizes specific conformations without enabling phosphotransfer [3].

In summary, q92519_ATP behaves as a stable, ligand‑bound pseudokinase that preserves a defined ATP‑binding pose while exhibiting increased flexibility in the activation‑loop region and reduced inter‑lobe communication relative to active kinases. The clustering of its dynamical descriptors with other pseudokinases confirms the functional classification derived from sequence analysis. To probe the potential regulatory roles of ATP in this system, we recommend site‑directed mutagenesis of the salt‑bridge partners (E312, D325, K337) followed by enhanced‑sampling MD or free‑energy calculations to assess the impact on pocket stability and ligand affinity. Such experiments could illuminate whether ATP acts as an allosteric modulator that influences downstream signaling pathways, as suggested for related pseudokinases [3].
