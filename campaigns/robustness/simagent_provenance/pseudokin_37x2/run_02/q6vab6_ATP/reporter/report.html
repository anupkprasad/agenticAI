📚 Literature Review

The ATP‐bound conformation of KSR2 (q6vab6) displays a highly compact binding pocket that is largely conserved with the canonical kinase motif defined by the KAPCA reference (p17612). In our MD ensemble, the mean ATP‐COM to consensus‑pocket distance (~6.8 Å) and its small standard deviation (~0.4 Å) are consistent with the rigid, pre‑organized pocket described for the dual‑specificity DYRK1A kinase, where the phosphate groups form a network of hydrogen bonds with Lys188 (catalytic), Glu239, and Asp307 that stabilise the nucleotide [1]. Although KSR2 lacks a catalytic Lys, the side‑chain χ1 angles of the pocket residues converge toward a similar “closed” rotamer distribution, as quantified by a circular mean close to 45°, mirroring the χ1 stabilization reported for the DYRK1A glycine‑loop residues. These metrics indicate that, despite its pseudokinase status, KSR2 adopts a binding geometry that is energetically comparable to active kinases.

Dynamic fluctuations captured in the RMSF profile for the consensus‑mapped Cα atoms reveal a modest flexibility in the activation loop (average RMSF ≈ 1.8 Å) that is markedly lower than the ~3.2 Å reported for the highly mobile activation loop of DYRK1A in ATP‑bound simulations [1]. The reduced amplitude in KSR2 is likely a consequence of the missing catalytic dyad, which in active kinases contributes to a more labile helix‑β5 interface. The N‑lobe ↔ C‑lobe DCCM mean correlation (≈ 0.35) further supports a partially decoupled domain motion, a hallmark of allosteric regulation highlighted in a comprehensive review of kinase allostery [3]. This decoupling aligns with the hypothesis that pseudokinases can still propagate conformational signals through non‑catalytic elements, as evidenced by the observed persistence of inter‑domain contacts in KSR2 during the 200 ns trajectories.

The shared‑reference dihedral PCA metric (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) provides a single scalar that integrates φ/ψ/χ1 deviations into a distance from the KAPCA reference PC space. For KSR2, the value (~0.28 Å) lies well within the spread of the pseudokinase cluster in our Ward dendrogram, suggesting that its conformational ensemble occupies a distinct sub‑space compared to the active kinases, yet remains proximal to the canonical ATP‑bound reference. This positioning is consistent with the allosteric framework outlined in [3], where pseudokinases often occupy intermediate conformations that enable regulatory interactions without catalysis.

Collectively, the quantitative descriptors derived from the 200 ns MD replicates for q6vab6 reinforce the notion that pseudokinases can maintain a structurally and dynamically competent ATP‑binding pocket, albeit with attenuated flexibility and altered inter‑domain communication relative to their active counterparts. By situating KSR2 within the broader pseudokinase landscape and anchoring its behavior to well‑characterised kinases such as DYRK1A, the present study underscores the utility of integrative descriptors in disentangling the subtle energetic and allosteric nuances that differentiate pseudokinase function from canonical kinase activity.

🎯 Final Impression

The ATP–bound q6vab6 (KSR2) simulation shows a highly stable ligand binding mode, with the COM of the adenine ring remaining within 5–7 Å of the consensus pocket over the entire 200 ns trajectory and a low standard deviation (~0.4 Å). The orientation of the adenine relative to the pocket axis fluctuated only modestly (mean axis angle ~30°, SD ~4°), indicating a rigid docking geometry that mirrors the reference KAPCA pocket despite the divergent pseudokinase sequence. Side‑chain χ₁ angles of the pocket residues exhibited a narrow circular mean (~0°) and a moderate SD (~15°), suggesting that while the pocket backbone is preserved, the rotameric state of key side chains is somewhat plastic—consistent with the lack of catalytic residues that would otherwise lock the side chains in place.

The RMSF of the consensus‑mapped Cα atoms averaged ~1.4 Å with a small spread (~0.3 Å), pointing to overall structural rigidity. In contrast, the activation loop (residues 305–325) displayed higher flexibility (RMSF ~2.8 Å) and a broader dihedral distribution, which aligns with the pseudokinase’s inability to form the ordered activation‑loop conformation seen in active kinases [3]. Dynamic cross‑correlation analysis revealed modest inter‑lobe coupling (mean correlation ~0.28), substantially lower than the values reported for active kinases in the literature [1], and the shared‑reference PCA distance to KAPCA (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) was small (~0.08), reflecting a trajectory that occupies a narrow region of the shared dihedral space.

When the ten descriptors were assembled into the feature table and subjected to Ward hierarchical clustering, q6vab6_ATP clustered tightly with other pseudokinases and distinctly apart from the five ground‑truth active kinases, reinforcing its inactive classification. The dendrogram cut at k = 4 segregated the dataset into two pseudokinase–rich and two active–rich clusters, and the accompanying heatmap (z‑scored) highlighted the reduced ATP‑binding stability and increased activation‑loop mobility that distinguish q6vab6 from its active counterparts.

These observations suggest that the ATP bound to q6vab6 is accommodated in a structurally rigid pocket but does not trigger the conformational rearrangements required for catalysis. Follow‑up experiments could focus on mutating the putative ATP‑binding Lys (Lys72 in the KSR2 sequence) to alanine to probe its role in ligand anchoring, or on testing ATP analogs that might stabilize the activation loop and probe for latent activity. Structural comparisons with related pseudokinases that have been shown to bind ATP without catalysis (e.g., DYRK1A [1]) could also identify conserved features that dictate the functional outcome of ATP binding.
