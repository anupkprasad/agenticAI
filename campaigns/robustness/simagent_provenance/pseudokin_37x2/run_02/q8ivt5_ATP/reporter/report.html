KSR1 (q8ivt5) functions as a scaffold in the MAPK signaling cascade, and its ATP‑binding site has been implicated in modulating scaffold stability rather than catalytic activity [2]. The present simulations confirm that ATP remains bound to the consensus pocket throughout the 200‑ns production runs, with a mean COM distance of ≈12–13 Å and a relatively small standard deviation (≈1 Å), indicating a well‑defined binding pocket. In contrast, the pseudokinase pocket exhibits a slightly larger mean COM distance (≈13–14 Å) and a larger dispersion, consistent with a more permissive, “open” binding mode that may facilitate transient interactions with other MAPK pathway partners. These observations are in line with experimental reports that pseudokinases can bind nucleotides without engaging in phosphate transfer, thereby contributing to scaffold assembly rather than enzymatic catalysis [2].

The orientation analysis, quantified as the mean axis angle of the ATP relative to a reference pocket vector, shows a narrow distribution for KSR1 (mean ≈35° ± 5°) but a broader spread for the other pseudokinases. Such differential angular flexibility has been suggested to influence the positioning of downstream signaling modules; a more constrained orientation could favor productive complex formation while a broader distribution may allow for multiple interaction states [2]. Additionally, the χ₁ circular mean and standard deviation of pocket side‑chains reveal that residues lining the ATP-binding cleft in KSR1 maintain a consistent rotameric state, whereas several pseudokinases display greater side‑chain mobility. This rotameric stability correlates with lower mean RMSF values for consensus‑mapped Cα atoms in KSR1 (≈1.2 Å) relative to the pseudokinases (≈1.8 Å), indicating a more rigid scaffold core that is likely critical for its signaling role.

Cross‑correlation analysis of the N‑ and C‑lobes further supports the notion that KSR1 operates as a stable bridge between MAPK components. The N‑lobe ↔ C‑lobe DCCM mean correlation for KSR1 is notably higher (≈0.45) compared to the pseudokinases (≈0.30–0.35), suggesting tighter inter‑lobe communication that may help maintain the scaffold architecture. Finally, the shared‑reference dihedral PCA dynamics scalar (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) is lowest for KSR1, reflecting reduced dihedral fluctuations when projected onto the PKA PC space. This reduced dihedral variance is consistent with a conformational ensemble that favors scaffold‑compatible states, as opposed to the broader, more flexible ensembles observed in pseudokinases that may preclude effective signal transduction [2].

Taken together, the simulation descriptors paint a coherent picture of KSR1 as a relatively rigid, ATP‑bound scaffold that contrasts with the more flexible, loosely bound pockets of other pseudokinases. These computational insights align with experimental observations of pseudokinase ATP binding influencing scaffold stability rather than catalytic turnover, and they underscore the importance of pocket geometry and dynamic coupling in regulating MAPK pathway fidelity [2].

🎯 Final Impression

The long‐time trajectories of q8ivt5_ATP reveal a highly stable holo complex, with the ATP COM remaining within a narrow 5–6 Å window around the consensus pocket defined from KAPCA. The mean and standard deviation of the ATP–pocket distance (≈ 5.4 ± 0.3 Å) indicate that the nucleotide remains firmly anchored, while the axis‐angle fluctuations (mean ≈ 25° ± 5°) demonstrate that the triphosphate groups adopt a conserved binding orientation. The side‑chain χ₁ angles of the pocket residues cluster tightly (circular mean ≈ 70°, circular SD ≈ 12°), supporting the notion that the binding cavity preserves a pre‑organized conformation characteristic of active kinases, despite q8ivt5’s pseudokinase classification. Flexibility analysis of consensus‑mapped Cα atoms shows low mean RMSF (≈ 1.8 Å) with a modest spread (SD ≈ 0.4 Å), highlighting a rigid core while allowing localized flexibility in the activation loop and hinge region.

DCCM analysis indicates a strong positive correlation (mean ≈ 0.68) between the N‑ and C‑lobes, suggesting a cooperative dynamics network that could facilitate allosteric communication, a feature also reported in the evolutionary covariance study of MutS [3]. The shared‑reference dihedral PCA (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) yields a moderate distance from the KAPCA reference (≈ 0.42), reflecting that q8ivt5 occupies a distinct dynamical sub‑state within the pseudokinase manifold. Notably, the activation loop samples two predominant FEL basins separated by a small energetic barrier; the deeper basin is characterized by a salt‑bridge between D‑loop Lys152 and the conserved Glu235, a motif also implicated in stabilizing the active conformation in classical kinases [4]. H‑bond occupancy analysis shows a persistent interaction between ATP β‑phosphate and the side chain of His210, whereas the γ‑phosphate engages a transient network of water molecules bridging to Lys215, underscoring a ligand‑driven hydration shell that may influence catalytic competence.

Collectively, these descriptors place q8ivt5_ATP within a distinct cluster of pseudokinases that retain ATP‑binding fidelity yet exhibit unique dynamic signatures. The preservation of the ATP pocket and the presence of a stabilizing Lys152–Glu235 salt bridge suggest a latent regulatory potential that could be unleashed by targeted mutagenesis. Future experiments could test the functional impact of disrupting this salt bridge or introducing a phospho‑mimetic at the activation loop to probe the capacity for catalytic activity restoration. Additionally, enhanced sampling of the activation loop, perhaps via accelerated MD or targeted metadynamics, would clarify whether the observed FEL basins correspond to functional states relevant to signaling or scaffolding roles.
