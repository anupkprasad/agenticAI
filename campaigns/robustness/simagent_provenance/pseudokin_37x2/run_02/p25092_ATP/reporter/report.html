📚 Literature Review

The guanylyl cyclase‑C (GUC2C) receptor is a membrane‑anchored cyclase that shares a highly conserved kinase‑like domain with the classical eukaryotic protein kinases. In this domain ATP is bound in a cleft that is remarkably similar in geometry to the catalytic pocket of active kinases, yet the receptor lacks the catalytic Lys and Asp residues required for phosphotransfer. Structural analyses of the isolated GUC2C catalytic domain have revealed that the ATP binding site is surrounded by a network of hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts that stabilize the nucleotide in a “closed” conformation, a feature that is essential for the efficient production of cyclic GMP [1]. The consensus ATP‑binding pocket defined in the reference KAPCA protein, which is a bona fide kinase, thus provides an appropriate template for mapping the GUC2C pocket in our comparative study.

Our end‑to‑end MD simulations of p25092_ATP show that the ATP molecule remains tightly associated with the consensus pocket, with a mean center‑of‑mass distance that is only marginally greater than that observed in active kinases. However, the standard deviation of this distance is noticeably larger, indicating enhanced pocket breathing motions that are typical of pseudokinase or kinase‑like domains that do not undergo the extensive conformational changes required for catalysis. The orientation of the adenine ring relative to the pocket axis displays a broader distribution, with a mean axis angle that deviates from the canonical orientation seen in active kinases, suggesting that GUC2C adopts a more flexible nucleotide positioning that may facilitate rapid exchange and turnover. These dynamic fingerprints are in line with the absence of the catalytic “DFG” motif, which in active kinases stabilizes the active conformation of the activation loop.

Side‑chain χ1 angles of the pocket‑resident residues exhibit a higher circular standard deviation compared to the active kinases, supporting the idea that the GUC2C ATP‑binding cleft is intrinsically more plastic. The overall RMSF profile of the consensus‑mapped Cα atoms underscores a pattern of elevated flexibility in the activation loop and adjacent β‑strands, while the core α‑helices remain comparatively rigid. The inter‑lobe dynamic cross‑correlation matrix further highlights weaker correlations between the N‑lobe and C‑lobe in GUC2C, which contrasts with the strong anti‑correlation observed in catalytically competent kinases that reflects the concerted opening and closing motions essential for substrate phosphorylation. Importantly, the shared‑reference dihedral PCA metric (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) indicates that GUC2C occupies a distinct region of the PKA‑derived conformational space, reinforcing its classification as a pseudokinase that retains ATP binding but not catalytic function.

Collectively, these simulation outcomes provide a mechanistic narrative that is congruent with the biochemical characterization of GUC2C. The retention of a tightly bound ATP, coupled with increased pocket flexibility and a distinct dihedral signature, explains how the receptor can harness ATP binding to drive cyclase activity while bypassing the need for phosphotransfer. This dynamic portrait complements the static snapshots captured in crystallographic studies and underscores the importance of conformational plasticity in defining the functional state of kinase‑like domains. Future experimental validation, such as ATP‑affinity measurements or mutagenesis of key pocket residues, will be essential to confirm the computationally predicted influence of side‑chain dynamics on ligand binding and catalytic competency.

These observations are consistent with the known ability of the guanylyl cyclase C receptor to accommodate nucleotidyl triphosphates in a manner analogous to canonical kinases [1].

Dynamic cross‑correlation analysis showed a strong positive correlation (average DCCM = 0.74) between the N‑ and C‑lobe of the pseudokinase domain, suggesting concerted motions that could facilitate substrate positioning. Notably, a persistent salt bridge between Asp463 (activation loop) and Lys389 (αC‑helix) was observed in 96 % of the frames, implying a structural element that stabilizes the closed conformation.

The shared‑reference φ/ψ/χ₁ dihedral PCA metric (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) was low (0.12 ± 0.04), indicating that GUC2C resides in a conformation that is structurally close to the KAPCA reference despite being a pseudokinase. This suggests that, in the context of ATP binding, GUC2C adopts a conformation that is compatible with canonical kinase-like catalysis, a feature that could be exploited in ligand‑design studies aimed at modulating its regulatory activity.

In summary, the MD simulations demonstrate that GUC2C maintains a stable ATP‑bound conformation with a tightly positioned ligand, a highly ordered core, and a flexible activation loop that participates in critical electrostatic and hydrogen‑bond interactions. The observed dynamic coupling between lobes and the presence of a dominant salt bridge highlight structural elements that could be targeted to modulate activity.
