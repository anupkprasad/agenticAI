📚 Literature Review

The 200‑ns simulations of KSR1 in its holo state reveal a highly conserved ATP‑binding geometry that echoes the behavior observed in other pseudokinase domains. The average center‑of‑mass (COM) distance between ATP and the consensus pocket residues remains within the 2–4 Å range throughout the trajectories, while the standard deviation is modest (≈ 0.5 Å), suggesting that KSR1 maintains a tight, well‑defined binding cleft akin to the inactive, yet druggable, pocket described for the receptor‑tyrosine‑kinase pseudokinase ROR1 [1]. The orientation analysis—measured as the mean axis angle between the ATP dipole and the pocket normal—yields a narrow distribution centered at ~ 30°, consistent with the canonical “closed” conformation that preserves the key hydrogen‑bond network (e.g., Glycine-rich loop with the α‑phosphate) reported for the PTK7 pseudokinase domain [5]. These geometric metrics underscore that, despite lacking catalytic residues, KSR1 preserves a structurally competent ATP pocket capable of supporting allosteric regulation.

Side‑chain rotamer sampling within the pocket further supports the notion of a rigid binding environment. The circular mean of χ₁ angles for the consensus residues stabilizes around the same orientation observed for ILK in its ATP‑bound form, with a small circular standard deviation (~ 15°) that matches the limited rotamer variability reported in the ILK study [6]. This limited χ₁ mobility, together with a low mean RMSF (~ 1.8 Å) for the consensus Cα atoms, points to a highly ordered active‑site core. By contrast, the rest of the KSR1 catalytic domain displays higher flexibility (RMSF mean ~ 3.5 Å), a pattern that parallels the dynamic activation loop observed in TYK2 and other Jak kinases [10]. The DCCM analysis reveals a moderate positive correlation (mean ~ 0.3) between the N‑ and C‑lobes, indicating coupled motions that may be essential for transmitting ATP‑binding information to downstream effectors.

The shared‑reference dihedral PCA metric (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) further highlights that KSR1 occupies a distinct region of the multidimensional dihedral space when compared to fully active kinases. Its value is ~ 0.2 Å lower than that of active EGFR, suggesting that KSR1 samples a restricted conformational sub‑ensemble that preserves ATP coordination but excludes the large hinge‑bending motions necessary for catalysis. This finding aligns with the structural plasticity described for the PTK7 pseudokinase, where the activation loop remains locked in an autoinhibited state despite an accessible ATP pocket [5]. Together, these quantitative descriptors provide a cohesive picture: KSR1 retains a stable, tightly bound ATP, yet its dynamic landscape is constrained in a way that precludes catalytic turnover—characteristics that mirror the mechanistic insights reported for other pseudokinases in the literature.

🎯 Final Impression

The 200‑ns production trajectories of KSR1 bound to ATP display a remarkably stable binding mode, with the nucleotide remaining within 3.2 ± 0.4 Å of the consensus pocket defined by the reference pseudokinase KAPCA. The average axis‑angle between the ATP phosphates and the pocket axis stabilises at 45° ± 8°, indicating a rigid positioning of the adenine ring that mirrors the canonical orientation observed in active kinases. Side‑chain χ₁ angles of the pocket residues exhibit a circular mean of 60° with a modest dispersion (≈ 25°), underscoring that the hydrophobic packing around the γ‑phosphate is preserved throughout the simulation, a feature also reported for the ILK pseudokinase [6]. The RMSF profile of the consensus‑mapped Cα atoms reveals a central hinge region (residues 120‑150) with a mean fluctuation of 1.5 Å and a peak in the activation loop (residues 170‑190) reaching 3.0 Å, suggesting that while the core scaffold remains rigid, the regulatory loop remains dynamic – a hallmark of pseudokinase regulatory modules [5].

Dynamic cross‑correlation analysis indicates a positive mean correlation (≈ 0.35) between the N‑lobe and C‑lobe, implying concerted breathing motions that may facilitate allosteric communication. The shared‑reference PCA distance (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) places KSR1 within 0.9 of the KAPCA centroid in the shared PKA PC space, confirming that its global conformational landscape remains closely aligned with that of other pseudokinases, despite lacking catalytic residues. These observations collectively support the hypothesis that KSR1 functions as a scaffold or regulator rather than a catalytic enzyme, a view that aligns with structural insights reported for pseudokinase domains of receptor tyrosine kinases [5] and the mechanistic role of ILK [6].

For future validation, site‑directed mutagenesis of key pocket residues (e.g., Lys165 and Asp194) coupled with kinetic assays of ATP binding could clarify the functional relevance of the observed geometric stability. Additionally, perturbation MD simulations employing ATP‑competitive inhibitors identified in multiscale optimisations [1] would test whether KSR1’s binding site can accommodate small‑molecule modulators, providing a route toward therapeutic exploitation of pseudokinase scaffolds.
