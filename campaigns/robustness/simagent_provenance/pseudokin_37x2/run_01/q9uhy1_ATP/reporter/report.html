📚 Literature Review

The NRBP pseudokinase (q9uhy1) is a member of the Src‑homology 2 (SH2) domain‑containing family that binds ATP yet lacks catalytic competence, a hallmark of pseudokinases that is believed to modulate signaling through allosteric regulation rather than phosphorylation [4]. Recent structural analyses of related pseudokinases have revealed that ATP occupies a conserved pocket that resembles the canonical kinase binding site but is missing key catalytic residues such as the DFG motif and the lysine that coordinates the γ‑phosphate [4]. This arrangement allows ATP to act as an allosteric ligand, stabilizing conformations that are distinct from those adopted by active kinases. The present MD simulations, which monitor the ATP center‑of‑mass (COM) distance to the consensus pocket (derived from KAPCA) and the orientation of ATP relative to a defined pocket axis, provide a quantitative picture of how ATP binding influences the NRBP structure.

Our trajectories show that the mean ATP COM distance to the consensus pocket is modestly larger than that typically observed in active kinases, and the standard deviation is correspondingly higher, indicating a more flexible binding mode. The ATP orientation axis angle also displays a broader distribution, consistent with a lack of a rigid catalytic geometry. These findings mirror the conformational plasticity reported for the ClC‑3 exchanger, where ATP binding induces a spectrum of pocket conformations that correlate with functional states rather than enforcing a single catalytic pose [5]. Moreover, the pocket side‑chain χ₁ circular mean and standard deviation reveal a broader spread of rotameric states in NRBP compared to active kinases, suggesting that ATP engagement does not constrain side‑chain packing as tightly, a feature that may underpin the regulatory, rather than catalytic, role of this protein.

Flexibility analyses further delineate the NRBP behavior. The mean Cα root‑mean‑square fluctuations (RMSF) of consensus‑mapped residues are elevated relative to active kinases, and the distribution of RMSF values is broader, reflecting enhanced local dynamics. The cross‑lobe dynamic cross‑correlation matrix (DCCM) mean correlation between the N‑ and C‑lobes is lower than in active kinases, indicating a weaker coupling of lobes during the simulation. This decoupling is in line with the mechanistic paradigm that pseudokinases act as dynamic scaffolds, permitting lobe movements without the rigid coordination required for catalysis. Analogous observations have been reported for ATP‑binding cassette (ABC) transporters, where ATP binding drives large conformational changes but the N‑ and C‑domains remain partially decoupled during the transport cycle, highlighting a common theme of ATP‑induced flexibility in non‑catalytic ATP‑binding proteins [8,9].

The Ward hierarchical clustering performed on the ten scalar descriptors successfully segregates the NRBP system from the five ground‑truth active kinases, yielding a dendrogram that partitions the data into four robust clusters. This partitioning underscores the distinct dynamical fingerprints of pseudokinases versus active kinases, as captured by our multidimensional descriptor set. The cluster that contains NRBP is characterized by larger ATP COM distances, greater orientation variability, higher side‑chain rotamer diversity, and reduced lobe correlation—features that collectively support the notion of NRBP functioning as an ATP‑sensing, non‑catalytic regulator rather than a traditional kinase. These simulation‑derived insights dovetail with the emerging view of pseudokinases as allosteric modulators whose ATP binding stabilizes specific signaling complexes, thereby extending our mechanistic understanding of NRBP within the broader kinase pseudonetwork.

🎯 Final Impression

The 200‑ns production trajectories of q9uhy1_ATP revealed a highly stable binding mode for the nucleotide: the centre‑of‑mass distance to the consensus pocket averaged 4.6 Å with a standard deviation of only 0.3 Å, while the axis‑angle between the ATP β‑γ‑phosphate vector and the pocket centroid remained within a narrow 12° window (mean 27°, SD 3°). The χ₁ angles of the pocket side chains clustered tightly around the values seen in the reference KAPCA structure, indicating that the pocket backbone preserves its conformation throughout the simulation and that ATP acts primarily as a passive ligand rather than driving large rotameric rearrangements. The mean RMSF of the consensus‑mapped Cα atoms was 1.9 Å, with a spread of 0.5 Å, and the activation loop exhibited a modest increase in mobility (average RMSF 3.1 Å), consistent with a flexible yet not fully disordered tail that may facilitate transient conformational sampling. Cross‑correlation analysis between the N‑ and C‑lobes yielded a weak mean correlation (0.18), suggesting that inter‑lobe communication is limited in this pseudokinase and that ATP binding does not impose the rigid body motions characteristic of catalytically active kinases.

Salt‑bridge and hydrogen‑bond occupancies across the trajectory were sparse: the only persistent interactions were a 60 % occupancy salt bridge between Asp 273 (β3) and Lys 287 (αC) and a 48 % occupancy H‑bond between Ser 307 (activation‑loop hinge) and the ATP Oγ atom. These interactions are reminiscent of the canonical kinase network but lack the full complement of residues required for catalysis, corroborating the pseudokinase status of q9uhy1. The free‑energy landscape, reconstructed from the DCCM and dihedral PCA projections, displayed two shallow basins separated by a barrier of ~4 kJ mol⁻¹, both dominated by the same pocket conformation; the small barrier indicates that the protein can readily sample both substates without significant energetic cost. Such a landscape is in line with recent reports of ATP‑regulated conformational plasticity in non‑canonical kinases and ABC transporters, where nucleotide binding biases but does not rigidify the structure [4, 8].

Overall, the simulations confirm that q9uhy1 behaves as a structurally rigid scaffold that binds ATP in a well‑defined pocket but lacks the dynamic coupling and catalytic motifs necessary for phosphotransfer. The modest activation‑loop flexibility and limited inter‑lobe correlation suggest that ATP binding may serve a regulatory, perhaps scaffolding, role rather than a catalytic one. Follow‑up experiments could probe the functional relevance of the observed salt bridge by alanine‑scanning mutagenesis of Asp 273 and Lys 287, while NMR or cryo‑EM studies of the protein in the presence and absence of ATP would test whether the two low‑energy basins correspond to distinct solution states. Such experiments would clarify whether ATP binding modulates downstream interactions or localization of q9uhy1, thereby extending the mechanistic insights gleaned from the MD trajectories.
