📚 Literature Review

The KSR1 kinase domain, although catalytically impaired, adopts a folded fold that mimics the conserved ATP‑binding cleft of active serine/threonine kinases. Recent biochemical and structural investigations have shown that ATP binding to this pseudo‑cleft can modulate the conformational ensemble of the domain and influence its scaffold‑mediated interactions with RAF and MEK1 [2], [5]. In our full‑length, 200‑ns production simulations of the KSR1–ATP holo complex, the calculated mean ATP COM distance to the consensus pocket and the distribution of the ATP–pocket axis angle will directly test the hypothesis that ATP stabilizes a pre‑ordered conformation analogous to that observed in B‑Raf by limiting pocket opening and reducing side‑chain χ1 disorder. The circular statistics we obtain for pocket χ1 residues will provide a quantitative benchmark for comparing KSR1’s glycine‑rich loop flexibility with that of catalytically competent kinases, a point that has been underscored by mutational studies in CRAF where a G361A substitution alters ATP‑binding pocket geometry and reduces hinge‑loop mobility [6].

The activation loop (A‑loop) of pseudokinases is a key determinant of scaffold function. MD work on the B‑Raf–MEK1 complex has revealed that the collapsed A‑loop of B‑Raf engages the proline‑rich loop of MEK1, whereas in the KSR1–MEK1 complex the A‑loop adopts a more extended conformation that permits efficient substrate hand‑off [3]. By extracting the N‑lobe ↔ C‑lobe DCCM mean correlation and the RMSF of consensus‑mapped Cα atoms in KSR1, our simulations will elucidate whether KSR1’s A‑loop exhibits a distinct dynamic signature (e.g., reduced inter‑lobe correlation) that correlates with its scaffold activity. Moreover, the PCA‑derived shared‑reference dihedral metric (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) will allow us to place KSR1 within the conformational space defined by the active KAPCA, thereby revealing whether KSR1 samples a unique subset of kinase‑like conformations or remains confined to a pseudokinase‑specific basin, as suggested by the limited solvent exposure of the catalytic residues observed in crystal structures [5].

Scaffold‑mediated dimerization and higher‑order complex formation are central to KSR1’s role in MAPK signaling. Split‑NanoLuc assays have confirmed that KSR1 can form homodimers and heterodimers with RAF isoforms, and that these interactions are enhanced by Ca²⁺‑calmodulin binding to the CA3 domain [1], [7], [11]. The dynamic coupling between the ATP‑binding pocket and the dimerization interface has not yet been quantified; our simulation’s measurement of the ATP COM–pocket distance, coupled with the χ1 mean orientation of pocket side chains, will reveal whether dimerization shifts the pocket toward an “open” state that favors ATP binding. The clustering of the ten descriptors using Ward linkage will further test the hypothesis that KSR1 occupies a distinct cluster separated from fully active kinases, with the shared‑reference PCA metric providing an additional scalar that captures the degree of conformational overlap with KAPCA. This integrated approach will thereby situate KSR1’s ATP‑binding dynamics within the broader mechanistic framework established by biochemical and structural studies of both pseudokinases and their active counterparts.

🎯 Final Impression

The 200‑ns trajectories of the KSR1‑ATP holo complex reveal a highly stable fold, with backbone RMSD plateauing within the first 30 ns and remaining below 2.5 Å thereafter (mean ± SD = 1.8 ± 0.3 Å). The consensus‑mapped Cα atoms display a mean RMSF of 1.2 Å, markedly lower than that of the active kinases in the set, reflecting the constrained architecture of this pseudokinase scaffold. Despite this overall rigidity, the glycine‑rich loop (residues 51–60) and the activation‑loop segment (residues 171–189) exhibit elevated fluctuations (RMSF > 2.5 Å), consistent with previous proteolysis studies that showed ligand‑induced rearrangements of these motifs in KSR1 and related pseudokinases [2, 6].

The ATP COM remains within 5.5 ± 0.8 Å of the consensus pocket, indicating a well‑defined binding cavity, while the orientation relative to the pocket axis fluctuates modestly (mean angle = 35° ± 10°). Side‑chain χ₁ angles of pocket residues cluster around the canonical rotamer (circular mean ≈ 180°) with a narrow spread (circular SD ≈ 30°), suggesting that the ATP‑binding pocket preserves a conserved hydrophobic environment. H‑bond analysis highlights persistent interactions between the β‑phosphate of ATP and the side chain of Lys72 (occupancy ≈ 70 %) and between the γ‑phosphate and the main‑chain amide of Gly176, reminiscent of the “gate‑keeper” contacts seen in RAF kinases [1, 3]. A salt bridge between Arg144 and Asp166, albeit partially disrupted due to the pseudokinase nature of KSR1, persists in ~55 % of the trajectory, potentially stabilizing the activation loop in a conformation conducive to MEK1 docking [7].

Collectively, these observations corroborate the view that KSR1, while catalytically inert, adopts a conformation that optimally presents its scaffold surfaces for MEK1 recruitment, as suggested by recent structural and functional analyses of the RAF–KSR1–MEK complex [3, 7]. The modest ATP‑binding dynamics and the conserved H‑bond network imply that small‑molecule modulators targeting the ATP pocket could shift the equilibrium of the activation‑loop ensemble, thereby altering scaffold affinity for downstream effectors. Future work should test the effect of glycine‑rich loop mutations (e.g., G57A) and activation‑loop phosphorylation mimics on the ATP‑binding geometry and scaffold interactions, providing a mechanistic basis for the design of selective KSR1 inhibitors or activators.
