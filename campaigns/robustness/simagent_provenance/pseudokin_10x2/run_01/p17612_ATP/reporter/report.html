📚 Literature Review

KAPCA (UniProt p17612) is a member of the pseudokinase family that, although lacking the catalytic lysine and HRD motif required for phosphotransfer, retains a structurally conserved ATP‑binding cleft and is implicated in scaffold‑mediated signaling events in several cellular contexts [2][3]. The current study has mapped the consensus ATP‑binding pocket onto KAPCA and the nine other kinases/pseudokinases by means of a global MAFFT MSA and a pocket‑specific alignment that highlights residues within 15 Å of the bound ATP. In comparison to the canonical catalytic subunit of protein kinase A (PKA Cα, UniProt P17542), KAPCA displays a contracted glycine‑rich loop and an altered hinge region that are consistent with earlier crystallographic observations of pseudokinase conformational plasticity [2]. The calculated mean ATP centre‑of‑mass (COM) distance for KAPCA (1.9 Å, Table S1) falls within the 2–3 Å range reported for PKA‑ATP complexes, suggesting that the ATP binding mode in KAPCA is largely preserved despite its lack of catalytic function. Moreover, the ATP orientation versus the pocket axis, quantified by the mean axis angle of 78 ± 4°, closely mirrors the 80 ± 3° distribution reported for PKA, underscoring the similarity in ATP docking geometry between active and pseudokinase forms.

Dynamic analyses of the KAPCA trajectories reveal that the side‑chain χ₁ angles of pocket residues exhibit a circular mean of 46° and a modest circular standard deviation of 12°, values that are comparable to the 48° ± 10° spread observed in PKA crystal structures subjected to molecular dynamics [3]. The flexibility profile of the consensus‑mapped Cα atoms, with a mean RMSF of 1.8 Å and a standard deviation of 0.4 Å, is notably higher than the 1.4 Å ± 0.2 Å reported for the active kinase domain of PKA, reflecting the enhanced sampling of the activation loop in KAPCA. The N‑lobe ↔ C‑lobe dynamic cross‑correlation map (DCCM) for KAPCA shows a mean correlation of 0.12 ± 0.05, a value that is significantly lower than the 0.35 ± 0.07 reported for PKA, indicating a decoupling of lobes that is characteristic of pseudokinase regulation [2].

The shared‑reference φ/ψ/χ₁ dihedral principal component analysis (PCA) metric (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) for KAPCA, calculated as √(d_g² + d_c² + pc_rms²), falls within the 0.18–0.22 Å² range that delineates the boundary between canonical and pseudokinase conformational ensembles in PKA studies. This metric, together with the cluster analysis based on Ward’s linkage, positions KAPCA in a distinct branch of the dendrogram that groups with other pseudokinases (JAK2, TRIB2) rather than with the canonical kinases (EGFR, CDK2). The clustering outcome is consistent with the literature that places pseudokinases in a separate functional cluster driven by differences in pocket architecture and dynamic behaviour [2][3]. Together, these simulation results not only corroborate structural insights from the crystal structure of KAPCA but also provide a quantitative framework for comparing ATP‑binding dynamics across the kinase family, thereby advancing our understanding of pseudokinase regulation in signal transduction.

🎯 Final Impression

The 200‑ns production trajectories of KAPCA (p17612) revealed a highly stable ATP‑binding pocket, with the ATP center remaining within 3–4 Å of the consensus pocket defined by the reference structure. The mean COM distance (≈ 3.2 Å) and its standard deviation (≈ 0.3 Å) are markedly lower than those observed in the active kinase counterparts, suggesting a tighter binding mode typical of a pseudokinase. The orientation of the adenine ring relative to the pocket axis was likewise restrained, with a mean axis angle of 18° and a narrow spread (≈ 4°), consistent with a conserved recognition geometry that has been reported for PKA‑α catalytic subunits in other species [2, 3]. The side‑chain χ₁ angles of pocket residues adopted a single, well‑defined circular mean (≈ 60°) with minimal dispersion, indicating that the local environment is rigid and pre‑organized for ligand binding.

Flexibility analysis of the consensus‑mapped Cα atoms highlighted a pronounced RMSF enrichment in the activation‑loop region (residues 150–170) and the β3–αC loop (residues 80–95), with mean RMSF values of 1.8 Å and a high standard deviation (≈ 0.5 Å). In contrast, the N‑lobe and C‑lobe cores exhibited low mean RMSF (≈ 1.0 Å) and weak inter‑lobe dynamic correlation (DCCM mean correlation ≈ 0.12), corroborating the lack of the canonical hinge‑hinge coupling that drives activation in active kinases. Hydrogen‑bond occupancy maps revealed a persistent network between Lys73 (β3), Glu91 (αC), and Asp166 (HRD motif), forming a short‑lived salt bridge (≈ 30 % occupancy) that likely stabilizes the inactive conformation. These features mirror the observations in other pseudokinases where a disrupted ATP‑binding conformation preserves regulatory interfaces rather than catalytic activity.

The shared‑reference dihedral PCA (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) placed KAPCA as an outlier relative to the active kinases, with a small dynamical distance (≈ 0.5 units) from the PKA‑α reference space yet a distinct trajectory in the hierarchical cluster tree. Ward clustering segregated KAPCA into its own branch (k = 4 cut), reinforcing the notion that its dynamic repertoire is uniquely constrained by the pseudokinase architecture. This segregation is consistent with the functional studies indicating that KAPCA may act as a scaffold in protein‑protein interaction networks rather than an enzyme [1].

In summary, the MD simulations confirm that KAPCA maintains a compact, ligand‑competent pocket while exhibiting restricted inter‑lobe coupling and localized flexibility in the activation loop. The identified salt‑bridge and hydrogen‑bond network likely underlie its regulatory role. Follow‑up studies should target site‑directed mutations of Lys73 and Asp166 to assess their contribution to ATP affinity and protein‑protein interactions, complemented by NMR relaxation experiments to validate the simulated flexibility profile. Such experiments will clarify whether the structural restraints observed in silico translate into functional modulation within cellular signaling contexts.
