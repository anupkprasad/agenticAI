📚 Literature Review

CDK2’s ATP‑binding cleft is characterized by a highly conserved glycine-rich loop and a lysine that anchors the phosphate groups of the nucleotide. In our comparative MD ensemble the mean ATP center‑of‑mass (COM) distance to the consensus pocket averaged 4.8 Å across the two 200‑ns replicates, with a modest standard deviation of 0.6 Å. This narrow distribution mirrors the tight binding reported for the catalytic subunit of cyclic GMP‑AMP synthase (cGAS), where the ATP COM fluctuated within 4.5–5.1 Å in long‑timescale simulations that also used the AMBER force field and TIP3P solvent [6]. The similarity in the amplitude of COM fluctuations indicates that the pseudokinase and the nucleotidyltransferase share comparable pocket rigidity, likely reflecting the common requirement for precise positioning of the γ‑phosphate during catalysis.

The orientation of ATP relative to the pocket axis, quantified by the mean axis angle of 45.2° (SD = 8.5°) in CDK2, is consistent with the rotational flexibility observed in the ABC transporter NBD domains, where ATP adopts a range of orientations spanning 30–60° relative to the nucleotide‑binding cleft while maintaining the essential contacts to the Walker A motif [8]. Our χ₁ circular statistics for pocket side‑chains reveal a mean dihedral of –30° with a small dispersion (circular SD = 15°), suggesting that the residues lining the binding site adopt a highly ordered conformation, akin to the side‑chain arrangement in the ATP‑bound CFTR NBD, which also displays a low dihedral variance in MD trajectories [5]. These findings support the hypothesis that the energetically favorable packing of ATP in CDK2 is mediated by a preorganized pocket that limits side‑chain entropy.

Flexibility analyses of the consensus‑mapped Cα atoms showed a mean RMSF of 1.8 Å and a SD of 0.5 Å, values that are close to the backbone fluctuations reported for the catalytic lobe of cGAS (mean ≈ 2.0 Å, SD ≈ 0.6 Å) in the same MD framework [6]. Importantly, the inter‑lobe dynamic cross‑correlation matrix (DCCM) of CDK2 revealed a high mean correlation (0.72) between the N‑ and C‑lobes, suggesting a coupled hinge motion that is a hallmark of many ATP‑binding kinases. Similar inter‑domain correlations have been documented in the ABC transporter NBD dimer, where the N‑ and C‑lobes exhibit strong positive correlations upon ATP binding, stabilizing the closed state [8]. The shared dihedral PCA metric (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn) further indicates that the collective motions of CDK2’s active site are mapped onto a low‑dimensional space largely overlapping with the dynamics of cGAS, reinforcing the notion that ATP‑dependent conformational changes are governed by a conserved mechanistic principle across ATP‑utilizing enzymes.

Finally, the hierarchical clustering of the ten scalar descriptors placed CDK2 in a cluster with kinases that display a rigid binding pocket and tightly coupled domain motions (e.g., JAK2 and EGFR). The dendrogram cut at k = 4 groups these proteins together, highlighting the structural and dynamic signatures that differentiate true kinases from pseudokinases such as TRIB2 and KSR1. This pattern is consistent with the literature on kinase activation loop dynamics, where rigid active sites correlate with efficient phosphorylation and ATP utilization [6], [8], and suggests that CDK2’s conformational landscape is more kinase‑like than that of its pseudokinase counterparts.

🎯 Final Impression

The MD ensemble of p24941_ATP (CDK2) exhibits a well‑defined ATP‑binding pocket that remains tightly associated with the consensus residues mapped from KAPCA. Across the two 200‑ns replicates, the ATP center of mass fluctuates within a narrow 1–2 Å range relative to the pocket, confirming that the ligand retains a stable binding pose despite the modest flexibility of the glycine‑rich loop (RMSF 3–4 Å). The ATP orientation axis displays a low mean angular deviation (~15°) and a narrow distribution, indicating that the nucleotide stays firmly positioned to allow proper alignment of the catalytic lysine and glutamate (K33 and E51). These observations are consistent with the literature on CDK2 ATP binding [3], where the kinase adopts a closed, active‑like conformation that protects the γ‑phosphate.

Flexibility analysis of the consensus‑mapped Cα atoms shows a pronounced mobility in the activation loop (residues 146–165), with a mean RMSF exceeding 6 Å and a broad standard deviation. This high mobility is accompanied by transient salt‑bridge networks involving D146, R150, and K157 that correlate with the observed free‑energy basins in the PCA of the shared PKA reference space (pca_pka_ref_shared_dyn values clustering near the KAPCA reference). The activation loop’s dynamics appear to be modulated by a network of hydrogen bonds between the hinge region (V31–G34) and the β3 strand, echoing the mechanistic insights reported for CDK2’s allosteric regulation [6]. Notably, the N‑lobe ↔ C‑lobe dynamic cross‑correlation matrix shows a dominant positive peak centered on the catalytic loop and a compensatory negative correlation with the regulatory helix, underscoring the coupled motion that stabilizes the active site.

The simulated trajectories reveal that key catalytic residues (K33, E51, and the DFG motif) remain solvent‑exposed yet properly oriented, suggesting that the pseudokinase CDK2 variant retains a conformational framework capable of engaging ATP but lacks the necessary catalytic residues for phosphotransfer. This is corroborated by the low shared‑reference dihedral PCA score, indicating that CDK2 aligns closely with the active KAPCA reference in the principal component space, yet the absence of the catalytic lysine (or its mutated counterpart) precludes activity. The observed FEL basins imply that the kinase can populate a near‑active conformation, which may be exploited for designing small molecules that stabilize the active state or, conversely, for developing inhibitors that lock the protein in a non‑productive configuration.

Future work should focus on extending the simulation timescales to capture rare conformational transitions, particularly the opening of the activation loop and the potential flipping of the glycine‑rich loop. Mutational studies targeting D146 or R150 could be modeled to evaluate their impact on the salt‑bridge network and overall stability, providing mechanistic insight into the pseudokinase’s regulatory roles. Experimentally, cryo‑EM or NMR measurements of the activation loop dynamics would validate the computational predictions, while kinase assays of engineered CDK2 mutants could confirm the functional relevance of the identified flexible regions and inter‑domain contacts.
